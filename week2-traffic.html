In 2017, during my years working at UCLA, I drove north on the 405 each weekday morning, heading for Westwood. The red lights were already there when my day began. Most mornings, I moved forward one car-length at a time.

From the driver’s seat, I saw only the taillights ahead. I knew millions of Angelenos were making their own way to work. We shared the same city and the same rush, though I could see only a few cars around me.

When I pull back, my commute becomes one small thread in a much larger pattern. Northbound and southbound, lane after lane, each car carries someone toward a day that matters to them.

This was my route to Westwood: the I‑405, running north–south through Los Angeles. LA Metro reports 289,000 vehicles a day through Sepulveda Pass. That is a daily total, not the number of cars in this drawing at one moment.

Because I drove alone, the diamond-marked carpool lane was closed to me. I watched a few cars move past in a quicker stream while I edged north, one car-length at a time. In Los Angeles, the freeway was a way of life. Buses and trains were not how most of the people around me got to work. Some colleagues began their days at four in the morning, just to get ahead of the traffic.

In 2017, Elon Musk’s Boring Company proposed a tunnel beneath Sepulveda Boulevard, parallel to the 405, carrying cars on electric skates. I imagine what that promise might look like: the pavement parts, and an elevator rises to take a car from the surface—not mine. It carries that car below the queue into an underworld of pods on electric skates, zipping left and right at absurd speed. Above, every car still inches north. Beside that velocity, our overground commute almost seems silly. My car stays in the line.

It is someone else’s car that disappears below. I am still here with everyone else, wondering what this new route changes for us.

A few cars peel away, one after another, and streak through the underworld. Above, the 405 looks exactly the same; my car has not advanced. LA Metro reports around 289,000 vehicles pass through Sepulveda Pass in a day—not all at once, but across the whole day. Imagine an elevator plucking a handful of riders from a packed commuter train in Japan and sending them down a private zipline. The train would still arrive almost as crowded as before. This drawing is not a capacity forecast. It asks a question of scale: can a fast route for a few change a commute shared by so many? In this imagined scene, no. The queue above does not thin.

And I still wanted to know what a ride would cost, who could reach it, and whether it would get anyone all the way home.

The questions I heard—and asked—were hard to shake: Who was this for? How many people could it really serve, at what capacity, and at what cost? Why this solution? Because it could be built? In 2018, Culver City Councilmember Meghan Sahli-Wells also questioned whether the Westside proposal would offer equitable access.

Culver City public comment · 2018 ↗

The Westside plan moved into the city approval process, then neighborhood groups sued over its environmental review. In November 2018, the Boring Company abandoned the proposal. It never became my way out of the morning line.

Among the Angelenos I knew, the announcement had made none of us excited. It left us asking: who would benefit, who would pay, and who would still be inching north toward Westwood?
