In 2017, during my years at UCLA, my mornings began with a drive north to Westwood. A sunny Los Angeles morning. The on-ramp curved ahead. I slipped into the queue, and joined everyone else going to work.

And yet, that time alone could be a solace. Before the daily chaos, the car was mine. My music. My volume. My own soundtrack to work. The morning commute was a ritual, not just a delay.

♪ ♫ ♪

When we stopped, I had time to look around. An expensive car beside an old one. People with very different lives, sharing the same few metres of asphalt. Occasionally, someone was singing with the abandon of a private karaoke room.

I kept an eye on the impatient drivers, too. A sudden move, an angry gesture. There was always someone trying to get ahead. I left a little space and stayed in my lane.

Complaining about the 405 was our daily punch line. We all knew the joke. This was how we navigated Los Angeles: through its veins, one slow pulse at a time.

This was my route to Westwood. Four or five ordinary lanes each way along much of the corridor, plus a carpool lane—and still nowhere to go. Around Sepulveda Pass, Metro reported roughly 300,000 vehicles a day in 2017. I was one of them.

The road was much bigger than my morning. From Long Beach, linked to the harbor by the 710, the 405 sweeps past the South Bay and Culver City, inland of Santa Monica. My exit was Westwood, for UCLA. Beyond it: the Getty, Sepulveda Pass, the Valley, and the connection to the 5. So many different destinations, threaded onto one road.

Metro · corridor geography ↗ · County boundary ↗

But the whole county was not what I needed to cross. I just needed to get to work. Back in my lane, the grand sweep of the map became a few metres of waiting.

Because I drove alone, the diamond-marked carpool lane was not for me. I envied those cars slipping past. But did I really want someone else in my little room on wheels? I wanted their speed, and my solitude. I could not have both.

My car was not an unusual choice. An earlier Census survey put nearly three quarters of LA County workers in the driver’s seat, alone. Some of my colleagues started at four in the morning, trying to beat a system that so many of us depended on.

But our cars were not the whole city. In 2017, Metro recorded about 290 million bus boardings and 113 million rail boardings. These were rides, not unique people. A city of solitary drivers also had a city of people travelling together.

I could choose the privacy of my car. Not everyone could. Metro’s rider research describes a public that is more likely to have low incomes, to be people of color, and to have fewer transport options than the county as a whole. My irritation and somebody else’s lack of choice were not the same thing.

We were unhappy, of course. We wanted something better. But the freeway already swallowed so much of the city. Where would another lane go? Among the Angelenos I knew, we loved our cars. Many of us would rather sit alone behind a steering wheel than squeeze into a commuter train like sardines. So we lived with it. An hour of my life, each morning, given to the road.

Then, in 2017, a billionaire announced a way out. Elon Musk would dig beneath Los Angeles: a network of tunnels carrying cars at 200 kilometres an hour. The ambition was audacious. To my ears, the confidence sounded pompous, too—as if one rich man could rescue us all from the morning line.

TED · April 2017 proposal ↗

He pictured Westwood to LAX in five or six minutes. I thought about my own hour behind the wheel. What would it feel like to get that time back? An hour becoming five minutes. It sounded almost like a magic trick. I kept coming back to the same question: how?

I imagine a patch of asphalt opening beside me. An elevator appears, takes a car from the surface, and lowers it into another Los Angeles. Down there, electric skates carry cars through the tunnels, flashing past at the promised speed. A private escape to somewhere else. The pavement closes. Up here, my hands are still on the wheel. The car taken underground wasn’t mine.

Los Angeles Times · 2017 ↗ · TED · concept explanation ↗

Another car disappears. Then another. The queue around me looks the same. I think of a packed commuter train in Japan: take a handful of people out and give them their own fast ride. What changes for everyone left inside? Beneath us, speed. Up here, the same morning.

And I still wanted to know what a ride would cost, who could reach it, and whether it would get anyone all the way home.

The questions I heard—and asked—were hard to shake: Who was this for? How many people could it really serve, at what capacity, and at what cost? Why this solution? Because it could be built? In 2018, Culver City Councilmember Meghan Sahli-Wells also questioned whether the Westside proposal would offer equitable access.

Culver City public comment · 2018 ↗

The Westside plan moved into the city approval process, then neighborhood groups sued over its environmental review. In November 2018, the Boring Company abandoned the proposal. It never became my way out of the morning line.
